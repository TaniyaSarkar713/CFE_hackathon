‘When there is love between a father and mother, the child will also be loved’: a qualitative exploration of the links between couples’ relationships, parenting and early childhood development in Mwanza, Tanzania
====================================================================================================================================================================================================================

Juliet K McCann, Alya Alsager, Alina Bhojani, Damas Joachim, Julieth Joseph, Mary Kabati, Joshua Jeong

ABSTRACT
--------
  Introduction
  The relationship between parents is a key component of the family system that is associated with various child outcomes. However, less is known about how caregivers and community members make meaning of the influence of couples’ relationships on child outcomes and to what extent family caregiving processes underlie these links in low-income and middle-income countries. We therefore aimed to qualitatively investigate the pathways through which positive and negative couples’ relationship dynamics influence early child development.

  Methods
  In June 2022, we conducted 60 in-depth interviews (29 fathers and 23 mothers and 8 community stakeholders) and 9 focus group discussions (3 with fathers only, 2 with mothers only and 4 mixed parent groups) from across four purposively sampled communities in Mwanza, Tanzania. We analysed data using elements of thematic content analysis and a grounded theory methodology.

  Results
  We identified five main mechanisms through which couples’ relationships influenced early child outcomes, including child emotional well-being, health, nutrition and development: (1) parents’ nurturing care practices, (2) parental violence against children, (3) parent–child bonding, (4) parental mental health and (5) child indirect exposures through witnessing interparental relationships. Many of these pathways were interconnected and shaped by gender norms.

  Conclusions
  Our results highlight the importance of engaging couples and targeting marital relationship dynamics as part of caregiving interventions that aim to improve early child outcomes. Strengthening family caregiving relationships between mothers, fathers, and each with their children is crucial for promoting early childhood development.



METHODS
-------

Study design
~~~~~~~~~~~~
These data were collected as part of a larger phenomenological study that aimed to understand the role of fathers in Mwanza, Tanzania.31 We conducted a secondary analysis of these data to newly investigate the influence of couples’ relationships on early child outcomes. The study incorporated both individual IDIs with fathers, mothers, community leaders, and community health workers as well as FGDs with mothers and fathers. Various respondents were interviewed through multiple data collection methods to triangulate results across different sources.

Study procedures
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
This study was conducted in four communities in Ilemela district, Mwanza, Tanzania. Ilemela district is a peri-urban setting of Mwanza city on the southern shores of Lake Victoria. Selection of the study communities occurred via a stratified sampling design to ensure the inclusion of two lake shore fishing communities and two inland communities. The final four communities were ultimately purposively selected in collaboration with our local study partner Tanzania Home Economics Organisation (TAHEA-Mwanza). Leaders in these communities then assembled a list of all caregivers in the community who met the following study eligibility criteria: adult biological parent who was aged 18–65 years; had a child younger than 2 years of age; was in a relationship with the child’s other biological parent; and resided in the same house as partner and child at some point during the past month. The community leaders then invited a random selection of participants from this list over the phone or in person for interviews and FGDs. All invited participants agreed to participate in this study and remained in the study for the full duration (one interview or focus group discussion). In each study community, we conducted in-depth interviews (IDIs) with fathers, mothers, community leaders and community health workers and focus group discussions (FGDs) with mothers and fathers. FGDs were with mothers alone, fathers alone and in a mixed group of mothers and fathers. These discussions ranged from 4 to 10 participants, with each of the mixed-group FGDs including at least 2 mothers and 2 fathers. Each participant was only sampled once for either an interview or FGD. Sampling continued until we reached thematic saturation, which was determined based on reviews of emerging findings and daily debriefs with the field team.

Data collection
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Semi-structured interview guides covered questions on couples’ relationships and parenting roles. Study tools were pretested and iteratively modified through piloting with mothers, fathers and community stakeholder in a non-study community. Data were collected in June 2022 by a team of five research assistants (including coauthor JJo) from Mwanza who were bilingual in Kiswahili and English. Three research assistants were men and two were women. While these research assistants had previously worked within the communities in this study, they did not have an existing relationship with any of the participants prior to this study or a strong personal bias about the research topic pertaining to couples’ relationships. All data collectors had prior professional experience relating to early childhood development and qualitative research, held a bachelor’s degree and received a 7-day training on qualitative research methods and interviewing skills. After obtaining written informed consent from all participants, IDIs and FGDs were conducted in Kiswahili in a private setting located centrally in the community. With the exception of caregivers’ young children who were welcome to attend the interview or discussion, there were no other individuals present during data collection besides the participants and the researchers.
IDIs and FGDs lasted approximately 60–90 min and 90 min, respectively. For IDIs, respondents were specifically asked if they had a preference on the research assistant’s gender, and the interviewer was matched according to their response. All interviews were audio-recorded then transcribed and translated into English by a team of experienced Tanzanian translators. Research assistants read aloud consent forms that described the purpose, risks and benefits of participating in the research. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data analysis
~~~~~~~~~~~~~
For this analysis, we specifically analysed the subsection of the topic guide devoted to couples’ relationships. For example, key questions from the topic guide included, ‘How, if at all, do positive things in your relationship with your partner impact your young child?’ and ‘How, if at all do negative things in your relationship with your partner impact your young child?’. We applied elements of a grounded theory approach with thematic content analysis to generate themes and identify pathways through which a couples’ relationships impacted young children.32 Grounded theory methodology supported the development of new theories that were rooted in the perspectives of participants and emerged through an inductive process.3234 Three research analysts independently coded the English transcripts against a structured codebook using ATLAS.ti V.22 and engaged in weekly meetings to discuss emerging themes and resolve any uncertainties. A subsample of 30% of the transcripts were randomly selected to be independently coded by another analyst and then compared with resolve any discrepancies. Through an iterative process involving discussions with the research team at Harvard University and TAHEA-Mwanza, themes were identified which then informed a conceptual model for how couples’ relationship dynamics affected child outcomes. In November 2022, findings were presented verbally during workshops in two study communities during which participants shared their feedback on the results.
The data analysis team included masters-level graduate students at Harvard University, a postdoctoral researcher at Harvard University at the time of the study, and NGO program staff based in Tanzania who supported the field research and data collection. All research analysts had prior research experience in parenting, couples’ relationships and early childhood outcomes, training in qualitative coding and analysis and field experience in East Africa.

Patient and public involvement
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Patients or the public were not involved in the design and conduct of this research.

RESULTS
-------
Our final sample consisted of a total of 121 respondents including 55 fathers, 58 mothers, 4 community health workers and 4 community leaders (table 1). The highest level of education for most parents was primary school, and the mean age of mothers and fathers was 27.0 years (range: 18–49 years) and 36.1 years (range 22–55 years), respectively.
We identified five distinct mechanisms through which couples’ relationships impacted young children in this community, which are summarised in figure 1. In the sections below, we summarise the key findings for each of these pathways, capturing the role of both positive and negative couples’ relationship dynamics on child outcomes, which encompassed children’s emotional, developmental, nutritional and health.

Marital conflict constrains nurturing care behaviours to promote child development
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The first mechanism underlying parental relationship dynamics and child outcomes was via parents’ nurturing care behaviours. Through this pathway, poorer parenting practices were described as impacting multiple child outcomes, including ECD, nutrition and health. First in terms of ECD, parents who described being in poor marital relationships (eg, frequent arguments, separation, physical violence) also discussed less engagement in play with their young children, which they recognised as having detrimental impacts on the child’s cognitive and socioemotional development.
As for child nutrition, mothers and community health workers described how marital conflict resulted in fathers refusing to provide and purchase necessary foods for their family, which was consequential for children’s healthy growth: ‘When you have a bad relationship, a child will not grow. A man will stop buying food and doing anything, a child health will be poor.’ (mother IDI, village 3). This mechanism was also observed in the context of positive couples’ relationships dynamics. A few parents and community leaders talked about how peaceful and supportive relationships were the foundation for more collaborative parenting that would improve children’s early development. Fathers in supportive partner relationships were more likely to engage in childcare and play with their young children, despite such activities being largely perceived as mothers’ responsibilities due to restrictive gender norms and primary expectations of fathers as providers. For example, one father described how positive marital relationships enabled men to provide more love for their families and engage more in play activities with their child: ‘If there is a good relationship, fathers play with children and if not, he won’t play with the children because he doesn’t love his family.’ (mixed FGD, village 2).

Marital conflict and violence increase parental violence against children, which can negatively impact children’s mental health
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The second mechanism linking couples’ relationship dynamics, and particularly marital conflict, to child outcomes was through increased parental violence and aggression towards children. Frequent arguments between couples and fathers’ perpetration of IPV was often connected to harsh disciplinary practices by both fathers and mothers against their children. Restrictive gender attitudes and norms were noted as legitimising men’s use of violence against their partners, with many fathers explaining violence was necessary to assert their authority and power in the household.
For some fathers, anger arising from marital issues led men to perpetrate physical violence against their children, which was often also exacerbated by men’s heavy alcohol use. While mothers did not similarly mention increased perpetration of physical violence against children as a result of partner conflict, a few mothers described speaking more harshly towards their children although unintentionally: ‘When I am in an argument with my partner, I get angry to children, I might even yell at a child who wants me to carry him. I think it’s wrong to treat children like that when we have quarreled.’ (Mother IDI, village 1). More violent treatment of children by fathers compared with mothers was consistent with parents’ attitudes that fathers’ harsh discipline was acceptable. Parental violence and aggression towards children in times of couples’ conflict were described as having negative consequences on children’s mental health: ‘When parents’ relationship is not good, it may affect children’s parenting because parents may beat and punish children unnecessarily just because they are in arguments with their partners. This affects children psychologically.’ (father IDI, village 1).

Couples’ relationship quality is linked with quality of parent–child bonding
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The third mechanism through which couples’ relationships impacted children was via the quality of parent–child bonding. This was discussed in the context of both positive (eg, fathers’ support for their partners) and negative couple’s relationship dynamics (eg, IPV). For example, one father described having greater love and a stronger bond with his child when he is in a positive relationship with his partner: ‘When our relationship is good, it makes a child love his father. When you get home a child happily runs for you to carry him.’ (father IDI, village 3). In contrast, for partners with a weak marital relationship, parents explained how this could negatively impact the relationship security between the father and child, with children becoming fearful or even feeling resentful towards their father. ‘A bad relationship between partners will make a child unhappy, seeing the father as an enemy and leading to hatred of a child to his/her father.’ (fathers FGD, village 4). Although the impacts of couples’ relationship was as commonly described with respect to mother–child relationship specifically, multiple respondents noted how marital conflict affected children’s feelings toward both parents equally.
Besides IPV, marital separation and fathers’ lack of support for their partners were also commonly cited by fathers and community leaders as weakening the child’s bond with the parent. Irregular and infrequent shared time between the child and their parent due to divorce or fathers’ disregard for their partners and families were noted as limiting opportunities for the child to bond and experience love from that parent. One community leader shared: ‘…when father and mother are not into good terms, a child will go with a mother. You cannot get a chance to see her and that makes children not recognise fathers because there is no bond and you are never there.’ (community leader IDI, village 2).

Couples’ relationships affect parental mental health, which in turn impacts child well-being
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Parents’ mental health was the fourth pathway connecting couples’ relationships to child outcomes. Marital issues, including frequent arguments and concerns about infidelity, commonly led to increased stress and unhappiness among both mothers and fathers. For a few mothers, their male partners’ lack of involvement and collaboration in childcare per traditional gender norms was underscored as an additional source of stress. Stress among both parents constrained caregiving capacities, which in turn, impacted other aforementioned mechanisms (eg, less responsive caregiving practices, weaker parent–child bonds). Many parents also perceived links between their stress or sadness and the happiness of their young child, as illustrated by this father: ‘When you are stressed it affects your child’s happiness; you won’t like to play with your child or build a good relationship with him/her.’ (mixed FGD, village 1).
On the other hand, couples’ relationships characterised by mutual respect and support fostered a sense of peace and joy among parents, which extended to the happiness of their children as well. For example, one mother shared: ‘When I am happy, laughing, a child will be happy. A child knows that today mother is happy and when you are sad the child knows that you are not okay. So when you are happy a child is happy.’ (mother IDI, village 1). Such happiness within the family was described as enabling greater parental engagement in activities that supported the young child’s development, such as play: ‘Interviewer: Can you tell me when you have good relationship, how does Goodluck benefit? Mother: He gets benefits of playing with his parents, if the parents are happy, he will also be happy.’ (mother IDI, village 4).

Witnessing of interparental conflict negatively impacts child mental well-being and their future relationships
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
The final mechanism linking couples’ relationships and child outcomes was an indirect pathway through children’s observations of their parents’ relationship dynamics. Many parents believed that when young children witness parental conflict or their fathers perpetrating physical violence and verbal abuse against their mother, children will become sad, ‘unpeaceful’ or behave ‘like she has been beaten’. As a result, several parents noted how they intentionally avoided arguing with their partners in front of the child. One mother described these impacts on a child’s mental health: ‘A child will be sad all the time and lacks peace when his/her parents constantly fight. When his/her father comes back home he/she becomes sad and it affects a child psychologically.’ (mothers FGD, village 3). A few parents and community leaders elaborated on how children witnessing conflict in their parents’ marital relationship caused them distress that impeded early child cognitive abilities and desires to learn and play. Intimate partner violence was justified by many fathers as a means of ‘discipline’ for their wives, with such attitudes shaped by patriarchal gender norms that appeared widely held throughout the community.
On the other hand, when children observed their parents in loving relationships, they were happy and peaceful, which parents and community health workers believed allowed them to engage more in play, be more ready for school, and ultimately support healthy ECD. For example, when asked how, if at all, a positive marital relationship impacts a child, one mother responded, ‘Children will be peaceful. A child sees that a father and mother love each other, so they become happy. If we play, they want to join. It builds them cognitively and being confident.’ (mother IDI, village 1).
Many parents and community leaders expressed concern that if their children observed negative couple’s relationship dynamics, such as violence and aggression, then they would adopt these behaviours in their own relationships later in life. For example, one father shared, ‘When children grow up and marry, they will take lessons from their parents. For example, if you as a father have a norm of beating your wife, your son will grow with that and will be doing the same. Children shouldn’t hear if there is a fight at home.’ (fathers FGD, village 2). Parents described how positive couples’ relationship dynamics also had later life benefits for when their children eventually became parents themselves. For example, one mother shared: ‘Marital relationships have positive impacts to children when partners love, respect and lead their children well. When children also get married, they will act as their parents did in staying peacefully in their relationships.’ (mixed FGD, village 1).

DISCUSSION
----------
The goal of this study was to explore how couples’ relationships influenced early child outcomes in Mwanza, Tanzania. Through interviewing mothers, fathers, community health workers and community leaders, we identified five distinct pathways through which a couples’ relationships impacted young children’s health, development and well-being. First, the level of partner support and coparenting was linked with both mothers’ and fathers’ parenting practices like play and other childcare activities. Second, violence in marital relationships appeared to carry over into parent–child relationships through specifically increased parental perpetration of physical or verbal violence against their children. Third, couples’ emotional and physical closeness affected the degree of bonding between a parent and the child, for example, with marital separation constraining children’s connections with their fathers. The fourth mechanism pertained to the role of parental mental health, such as on caregiving and child bonding. Lastly, child outcomes were also influenced indirectly through children witnessing marital dynamics between parents.
Several of these identified links between couples’ relationship dynamics and child outcomes have been well-examined in prior studies. This is especially the case regarding the impact of parental IPV on young children. For example, multiple reviews have established the co-occurrence and associations between mothers IPV victimisation and maternal violence against children.35 36 We extend this evidence by revealing that fathers’ perpetration of IPV can also exacerbate fathers’ harsh punishment. Supporting the parental mental health mechanism, prior quantitative studies have demonstrated the potential mediating role of maternal stress, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms in the relationship between IPV and child outcomes.3739 Similarly, in a qualitative study in Timor-Leste, mothers commonly described how their experiences of IPV led to episodes of explosive anger, which was often accompanied by harsh parenting.27 Regarding the role of parent–child bonding, a qualitative evaluation of a violence-prevention intervention in Uganda found that participating couples who reported reductions in IPV also expressed improvements in parenting and discipline practices, which contributed to stronger parent–child relationships.40 Lastly, with respect to our final identified mechanism, a qualitative study in Rwanda described the consequences of children witnessing IPV in their family, including the direct impacts of exposure to violence on children’s emotional well-being.26
While most existing evidence has focused on negative dynamics and specifically IPV, relatively few studies in LMIC settings have equally considered positive marital relationship dynamics and how that too may influence caregiving and child outcomes. In one qualitative study with mothers and fathers in the Lake Zone region of Tanzania, parents recognised the importance of positive communication and cooperation in their relationship for their child’s healthy growth.41 Another qualitative study in Tanzania and Zimbabwe found that fathers were more likely to engage in childcare if they were in stronger, more emotionally intimate relationships with their partner.25 We build on these previous studies by identifying the pathways that explain and underlie these links and particularly with respect to early child outcomes.
Much of the existing literature regarding the mechanisms between positive couples’ relationships and children’s outcomes comes from high-income countries (HICs). These studies have underscored the central mediating role of more positive parenting, improved parent–child interactions and reduced parental stress.84245 Our study provides confirmatory evidence for a similar theory of change in the Tanzanian context to suggest cross-cultural benefits of positive couples’ relationships in global contexts. Future research on couples’ relationships and child outcomes especially in LMICs should dually examine how both positive and negative aspects of marital relationships influence young children and consider both behavioural and psychosocial mechanisms.
Although our study focused on early child outcomes, many parents and community stakeholders also believed that children would grow up to model the same behaviours observed between their parents’ marital relationships in their own future partner relationship as adults. Such intergenerational effects are well-substantiated in the existing literature. For example, a multinational survey with men in eight LMICs found that witnessing parental violence was the strongest risk factor for men ever perpetrating physical violence against their partner.46 The life course benefits of positive relationships is also supported in prior literature from HIC settings pertaining to the links between marital satisfaction and positive interparental conflict resolution and children’s own relationship satisfaction and affection later life.47 48
It is worth also recognising how our findings were rooted within the gender attitudes and norms of the local context. Restrictive gender norms contributed to many of the negative relationship dynamics described, including men’s use of violence against their partners as a means of uploading patriarchal family dynamics. At the same time, we found that experiences of positive couples’ relationships including shared decision-making and peaceful conflict resolution were facilitated by more gender-equitable attitudes, especially on the part of fathers. Our findings are in line with prior studies that have explored the links between gender attitudes and couples’ relationships in sub-Saharan Africa, including the role these norms play in condoning IPV24 47 and the associations between more gender-equitable norms and positive communication in relationships.49 Many of the mechanisms, including through caregiving practices and violence against children, were also influenced by gender attitudes and norms. In this community, fathers were not typically involved in childcare and were reported as using more serious and harsh discipline compared with mothers.31 Further, fathers’ lack of partner support in childcare activities was frequently noted as a source of stress for some mothers, which was another pathway through which children were impacted. The influences of gender norms on parenting practices, discipline and mental health have been similarly documented in other studies and contexts.50 51 Overall, given the influence of gender norms on both couples’ relationship dynamics and the mechanisms linking these dynamics to child outcomes, couples’ and caregiving interventions are likely to be more impactful by additionally targeting gender attitudes that perpetuate restrictive masculinity.50
There are several notable strengths of this study. The relatively diverse sample that included mothers, fathers, community health workers and community leaders allowed for many perspectives to be captured in our results. We also approached this investigation on the impacts of couples’ relationships on young children in terms of not only negative relationship dynamics like IPV, but also positive relationship dynamics, which provided a more holistic perspective to this exploration. At the same time, this study had some limitations. The topic guide was framed to understand the family caregiving experiences of mothers and fathers and other topics besides exclusively couples’ relationships. Thus, we did not probe extensively about the contributing causes to positive and negative marital relationships or why caregivers perceived different mechanisms as underlying the links with parenting and child outcomes. Moreover, we considered child outcomes broadly, and we did not specifically explore whether the effects of couples’ relationship dynamics may have varied depending on the types of child outcomes (eg, ECD vs nutrition). Given the sensitive nature of many of the topics discussed, including about IPV and violence against children, parents’ responses may be subject to social desirability bias. Finally, our results are from a peri-urban setting in Mwanza, Tanzania, and thus community perspectives may differ in other settings of Tanzania.

CONCLUSION
----------
This study triangulated the perspectives of various community stakeholders to identify five pathways by which couples’ relationships influence young children in Mwanza, Tanzania. Overall, more positive interparental relationships enhanced early child outcomes. Our results highlight the importance of embracing family-systems approaches, engaging couples jointly and leveraging community support systems in interventions seeking to improve child outcomes. Incorporating strong behavioural and psychosocial programme components to target the multiple potential pathways underlying these links will be especially needed. Finally, future programmes should additionally integrate gender-transformative approaches to dismantle restrictive gender norms that contribute to marital conflict and promote more positive male partner engagement and equitable family caregiving of young children.
Globally, approximately 250 million children under 5 years of age (43%) are estimated to be at risk of poor developmental outcomes.1 Various factors can compromise early childhood development (ECD), including suboptimal feeding practices and malnutrition, illness, harsh parenting and exposure to family violence.2 3 Caregivers play one of the most important roles in providing nurturing care and ensuring safe and supportive family environments to promote optimal ECD.2 However, the vast majority of research on caregiving relationships for young children has focused on mother–child relationships.4 This selective focus on the mother–child dyad overlooks the influence of other important aspects of the family system that can additionally shape children’s well-being.5 6
The relationship between a child’s mother and father, or couples’ relationships, is one key dimension of children’s family environments that can influence child outcomes.7 Several theoretical frameworks motivate these links. For example, family systems theory describes how the family unit contains multiple subsystems (eg, mother–father, mother–child, father–child networks) that are interconnected and interactively shape one another.8 9 Another theoretical framework, the spillover hypothesis, suggests that the affect and behaviour from one family relationship can be transmitted to other family relationships.10 For example, this theory has been used to explain associations between fathers’ perpetration of intimate partner violence (IPV) and violence against their children.11 12 Furthermore, emotional security theory describes how interparental conflict can threaten children’s emotional well-being both via direct exposures and indirect effects via poorer parenting practices.13 14
A growing body of global evidence has demonstrated links between couples’ relationships—both positive and negative dynamics—and child outcomes. One cohort study in the USA found that higher quality marital relationships predicted higher levels of parental engagement among both mothers and fathers throughout early childhood.15 In Germany, parental relationship satisfaction was longitudinally linked to improved infant personal–social development.16 Finally, shared decision-making between partners was associated with improved breastfeeding practices and child nutritional outcomes in India.17 Research has also revealed how negative couples’ relationship dynamics can be risk factors for child outcomes. For example, studies using nationally representative data sets from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have documented associations between IPV and stunting,18 child mortality19 and poorer ECD outcomes, with the impact on ECD outcomes mediated by lower levels of maternal and paternal stimulation.20
Despite these multiple motivating theoretical frameworks and a growing evidence base supporting links between couples’ relationships and child outcomes, there have been limited qualitative investigations unpacking how caregivers ascribe meaning to these concepts based on their lived experiences. This evidence gap is especially stark in LMIC settings, where households and communities are disproportionately affected by poverty, unemployment, and other stressors that significantly constrain family caregiving relationships.21 Moreover, considering how family relationships are embedded within social, cultural and gender norms, it is crucial to examine how these contextual factors may play a role in shaping caregiving dynamics in different populations around the world.22 For example, hegemonic masculinity and men’s power over women are widely accepted norms in many settings across sub-Saharan Africa,23 24 which further underscores the importance of qualitatively investigating these family processes in diverse cultural contexts.
There are a few notable exceptions of qualitative studies on this topic from LMICs. In one study in Tanzania and Zimbabwe, men and women described how stronger emotional intimacy within couples’ relationships was a key enabling factor that encouraged male engagement in household chores and childcare activities.25 In terms of partner violence specifically, a qualitative evaluation of an IPV prevention programme in Rwanda described the negative consequences of IPV on children including on their development, mental health and food security.26 Another study in Timor-Leste qualitatively described the role of IPV on maternal mental health problems, specifically resulting in more explosive anger against children and harsh parenting.27 However, much of this literature on couples’ relationships has focused on IPV specifically. Relatively few studies have holistically explored how both negative and positive aspects of marital relationships influence child outcomes, with even fewer unpacking the potential mechanisms through which couples’ relationships relate to child outcomes.
The objective of this study was to qualitatively explore how couples’ relationship dynamics positively and negatively influenced young children and the care they were provided in Mwanza, Tanzania. Specifically, we sought to generate a conceptual model that outlines the distinct pathways through which a couple’s relationships may influence child outcomes, broadly defined in terms of ECD, nutrition and health outcomes. It is also worth noting that most prior studies in this literature on couples’ relationships and caregiving of young children has come from the perspectives of mothers,28 29 with few studies purposefully interviewing fathers.30 We sampled both mothers and fathers equally and triangulated across their perspectives to obtain a fuller understanding from across multiple viewpoints. These findings can inform the design of future couples’ and parenting interventions aimed at improving ECD outcomes in the local context.
