Witnessing of interparental conflict negatively impacts child mental well-being and their future relationships The final mechanism linking couples’ relationships and child outcomes was an indirect pathway through children’s observations of their parents’ relationship dynamics. Many parents believed that when young children witness parental conflict or their fathers perpetrating physical violence and verbal abuse against their mother, children will become sad, ‘unpeaceful’ or behave ‘like she has been beaten’. As a result, several parents noted how they intentionally avoided arguing with their partners in front of the child. One mother described these impacts on a child’s mental health: ‘A child will be sad all the time and lacks peace when his/her parents constantly fight. When his/her father comes back home he/she becomes sad and it affects a child psychologically.’ (mothers FGD, village 3). A few parents and community leaders elaborated on how children witnessing conflict in their parents’ marital relationship caused them distress that impeded early child cognitive abilities and desires to learn and play. Intimate partner violence was justified by many fathers as a means of ‘discipline’ for their wives, with such attitudes shaped by patriarchal gender norms that appeared widely held throughout the community. On the other hand, when children observed their parents in loving relationships, they were happy and peaceful, which parents and community health workers believed allowed them to engage more in play, be more ready for school, and ultimately support healthy ECD. For example, when asked how, if at all, a positive marital relationship impacts a child, one mother responded, ‘Children will be peaceful. A child sees that a father and mother love each other, so they become happy. If we play, they want to join. It builds them cognitively and being confident.’ (mother IDI, village 1). Many parents and community leaders expressed concern that if their children observed negative couple’s relationship dynamics, such as violence and aggression, then they would adopt these behaviours in their own relationships later in life. For example, one father shared, ‘When children grow up and marry, they will take lessons from their parents. For example, if you as a father have a norm of beating your wife, your son will grow with that and will be doing the same. Children shouldn’t hear if there is a fight at home.’ (fathers FGD, village 2). Parents described how positive couples’ relationship dynamics also had later life benefits for when their children eventually became parents themselves. For example, one mother shared: ‘Marital relationships have positive impacts to children when partners love, respect and lead their children well. When children also get married, they will act as their parents did in staying peacefully in their relationships.’ (mixed FGD, village 1).
